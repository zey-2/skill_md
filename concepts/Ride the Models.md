# Ride the Models

## Summary

"Ride the models" is Dan Shipper's career survival thesis: the only durable strategy for staying employable as AI capabilities accelerate is to continuously play with new models as they arrive, apply them to real work, and discover new uses before they become mainstream.

Source: `raw/The AI paradox More automation, more humans, more work  Dan Shipper.md`.

## The Core Strategy

Shipper's prescription is not a specific tool or workflow — it is a stance:

- **When a new model arrives, turn the rock over again.** Test whether it can now do what it couldn't last time.
- **Apply it to whatever you care about** — your job, side projects, personal tasks.
- **Stay curious and playful rather than afraid.** FOMO-driven adoption is less effective than genuinely enjoying the discovery process.
- **Find your "moment of joy"** — the first time AI does something surprising and useful for you personally. That is what sustains long-term engagement.

## Why This Works

The structural reason riding the models works is that AI makes **yesterday's human competence cheap**. Models ingest existing data and deploy it at near-zero marginal cost. This commoditizes skills that were previously valuable. But humans who ride the models use that frozen competence as raw material to create new, situation-specific expertise — and that new expertise has not yet been incorporated into the next model release.

This creates a moving target: models always trail the people who push them into novel territory. The gap closes only when that novel usage becomes data for the next training run. By then, riders have already moved further ahead.

## The Benchmark Connection

Shipper illustrates this with his senior engineer benchmark. He built a test app (Proof) via vibe coding, then asked models to rewrite it from first principles. Models scored 30/100; human senior engineers scored 80s-90s. When GPT-5.5 arrived, it scored 62 — a 30-point jump.

But the deeper insight is that **it would be easy to change the benchmark to zero out the current model** once it saturates the current framing. Humans can always reframe the problem to a higher level. This means benchmark saturation does not equal capability parity — it means the human has found a new bar.

## Where the Edge Actually Is

> "I think the edge of AI is wherever AI meets a real human doing something."

Shipper argues that the frontier of AI usage is not in San Francisco among model builders. Model makers don't necessarily know how to use their own products effectively. The real edge is wherever an individual practitioner applies a model to their specific situation for the first time. Because models are broadly accessible (not locked behind enterprise procurement), anyone with curiosity can be at the frontier.

## What Companies Get Wrong

Many organizations handicap their employees by restricting access to the latest models, requiring approval workflows, or mandating specific approved tools. Shipper suggests that if your company doesn't allow it, you should still experiment on your own time. The competitive advantage of riding the models compounds over time, and waiting for organizational permission puts you behind.

## Practical Tactics

- Try all workflows in Codex or Claude Co-work to see how the work surface changes.
- When a new model drops, re-test tasks that previously failed.
- Use both major platforms (OpenAI and Anthropic) for different strengths — don't lock into one.
- Share discoveries with others; collective exploration accelerates individual learning.

## Model Selection Heuristic

Felix Rieseberg provides a practical heuristic for choosing between model tiers: **reach for Opus when you don't yet know what you're asking for; Sonnet is sufficient for well-scoped problems.** The distinction is not about technical complexity but about problem decomposition. A lawyer, accountant, or creative professional's value lies partly in reinterpreting what the client actually needs — Opus excels at that reframing work. For concrete tasks ("read this floor plan, add units, make a new one"), Sonnet 4.6 is "very very good" and rarely bumps into its ceiling.

For most people, Rieseberg suggests not thinking about model selection too deeply — Sonnet handles the majority of daily tasks well. The model choice becomes relevant when the problem itself is ambiguous and the value is in figuring out what question to ask.
