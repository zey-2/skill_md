# How to Get Your Company AI Pilled

**Source**: X post, `https://x.com/geoffintech/status/2042002590758572377`
**Author**: @geoffintech
**Published**: 2026-04-08
**Created**: 2026-05-18

## Summary

This post presents Ramp's AI adoption as a culture, tooling, and operating-model problem rather than a strategy-deck problem. The core claim is that company-wide AI fluency compounds when leadership sets expectations, removes access constraints, gives employees a stage to show work, provides deeply integrated internal agents, and treats AI proficiency as a learning curve that everyone is expected to climb.

## Key Points

- Ramp claims 6,300% year-over-year AI usage growth, 99.5% team activity on AI tools, 84% weekly coding-agent usage, and more than 1,500 internal apps shipped in six weeks by over 800 builders.
- Non-engineers are described as meaningful production contributors, accounting for 12% of human-initiated production PRs through Ramp Inspect.
- The adoption model started with leadership clarity, an AI guild, Slack sharing channels, all-hands demos, and usage tracking rather than a formal training curriculum.
- Ramp frames AI proficiency as a ladder: basic chat use, custom GPTs and early tools, job-automation apps and code contributions, then systems builders who lift everyone else.
- Internal tools are expected to have short shelf lives. The source treats tool obsolescence as healthy when people stay attached to problems rather than current implementations.
- Ramp's org model combines a central platform team with functional teams that build local workflows on top and feed roadmap pressure back to the center.
- Visibility is a major adoption lever: Slack channels, office hours, onboarding, demos, early-convert spotlights, and leaderboards turn use into a public cultural norm.
- Glass, Ramp's internal Claude Cowork-style agent, auto-configures through SSO and connects to many workplace systems so non-engineers can reach an "aha" moment quickly.
- Dojo, a skills marketplace, lets employees package and share workflows; the source says over 350 skills were shared company-wide.
- Ramp removes budget, token, role-based access, and connector constraints, treating AI usage as an exploratory learning budget.

## Evidence

- The source lists specific adoption metrics: 99.5% active team usage, 84% weekly coding-agent usage, and 1,500+ internal apps shipped in six weeks.
- Glass is described as auto-configuring through Okta SSO and connecting to Salesforce, Snowflake, Gong, Slack, Notion, Google Workspace, and Figma.
- Examples of non-engineer use cases include a risk analyst automating financial modeling, a sales ops lead replacing a compensation model, an L&D lead building a simulator, and finance building a contract reviewer.
- The source names concrete adoption rituals: `#ramp-uses-ai`, Friday office hours, AI onboarding, all-hands demos, and leaderboards.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The source is intentionally aggressive about mandatory AI adoption and states that employees stuck at basic use without self-starting may not remain at the company. This creates a tension with slower learning curves, accessibility, role variation, and the risk of optimizing for visible usage over durable productivity.
- Ramp celebrates creative destruction and short tool shelf lives, but the source leaves open how the company prevents maintenance debt, duplicated systems, and security issues as many non-engineers build.

## Open Questions

- How are quality, security, and ownership enforced for thousands of non-engineer-created tools and PRs?
- Which leaderboard metrics correlate with durable business outcomes rather than performative activity?
- How transferable is Ramp's approach to companies without a high-velocity culture or strong engineering/product coaching capacity?
