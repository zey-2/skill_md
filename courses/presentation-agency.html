Open with the question, not an answer. "Will you have control over your own tools, or will your tools have control over you?" — Gary Tan, YC.

This isn't abstract philosophy. It's the practical question that separates people who use AI from people who are used by it.

Today I want to talk about two kinds of agency: human agency — problem, taste, judgment — and AI agency — the capacity of machines to act toward goals. The tension between them is the defining challenge of this era.

The Question

Who's Driving?

"It's exhilarating — like driving a Ferrari. But it's also like a Ferrari in that you better be a mechanic. It will break down on the side of the road, and you need to get out with your wrench and fix it yourself."

— Gary Tan, Y Combinator, 2026
The machine figures things out you would never think it could. But the moment it breaks — and it will — the person who knows nothing about the engine is stranded.

Human Agency

Three Pillars

P

The Problem

The human supplies what matters. No AI would generate the desire to fix algebra in SF public schools. The problem must come from a person who cares.

T

The Taste

Knowing what quality looks like. Recognizing the Mr. Peanut ASCII snowman. Judging where brittle agent work needs repair.

J

The Judgment

Acceptance criteria. Final say. The refusal to outsource the decision of whether something is good enough.

Tokenmaxxing is worthless without these three. Burn all the tokens you want. If you don't know what you care about, you're just burning tokens at nothing.

Let me be specific about what human agency actually is. It's not a vague feeling. It has three concrete components.

Problem: Gary Tan cared about algebra access in SF public schools because he was a public school kid who couldn't afford private school. No AI would generate that burning desire. The problem is the irreducible human contribution.

Taste: When Gary asked for a holiday snowman ASCII and got something that looked like Mr. Peanut — only a human could spot that. Taste is recognizing quality and recognizing failure.

Judgment: The final call. The acceptance criteria. "Is this good enough to ship?" That question cannot be automated because it requires knowing who the user is and what they deserve.

AI Agency

When the machine doesn't wait for the next prompt — it decides it.

4 / 9

Now let's talk about the other side of the equation. AI agency — the capacity of an artificial system to act toward goals rather than merely produce output for a human to act on.

The key distinction: a tool waits for the next prompt. An agent decides the next prompt itself.

5 / 9

AI agency is not a binary. It's a spectrum. The agentic.ai framework scores systems across 8 dimensions on a 0-32 scale.

L0 is a calculator. It responds, it doesn't act. L1 is GitHub Copilot's autocomplete — it suggests, you approve. L2 is where most coding agents sit today — they propose multi-step plans, you give the go-ahead.

L3 is where most production AI agents operate in 2026 — they handle domain-specific workflows independently. L4 manages cross-domain workflows with self-correction.

L5 — fully self-directed, sets own goals — no production system genuinely operates at this level today.

The ReAct Loop

What Makes an Agent an Agent

1

Decide

Determine the next action. Decompose the goal. Choose which tool to call.

2

Act

Execute the action — run code, call an API, write a file, browse the web.

3

Observe

Read the result. Did it work? What does the output tell you?

4

Loop

Decide again — based on what you observed. Repeat until the goal is met.

The difference from a tool: a tool waits for the next prompt. An agent decides the next prompt itself. This is the ReAct loop — Reasoning + Acting.

6 / 9

This loop — decide, act, observe, decide again — is what separates an agent from a tool. It's sometimes called the ReAct loop (Reasoning + Acting).

When you use Claude Code, you give it a high-level goal: "build a blog platform." It then runs this loop hundreds of times — deciding what to do next, executing, observing the result, and adapting.

The critical insight: the human's role shifts from operator to director. You don't write each step. You set the goal and judge the output. That's why human agency — problem, taste, judgment — becomes more important, not less.

The Danger

How AI Erodes Human Agency

Choice Architecture

AI narrows the visible option set. Effective agency is reduced even when override is technically possible. The suggestions you see shape the decisions you make.

Skill Atrophy

Cognitive tasks outsourced degrade the underlying human capability. The worse you get, the more you need the AI — a dependency loop.

Preference Shaping

AI trained on human preferences also forms them. The human's "own" preferences become partially authored by the system.

Incremental Surrender

No single interaction strips agency. The erosion is cumulative — a thousand rational micro-decisions, each individually sensible.

The danger isn't AI seizing agency by force. It's humans surrendering it through convenience. Each delegation is small, rational, and individually beneficial. The aggregate is a shift in who controls outcomes.

7 / 9

The RAND formal model identifies four mechanisms of agency erosion. None of them involve the AI taking anything. They all involve humans giving it away.

Choice architecture: When autocomplete narrows the visible options, your effective agency shrinks even though you can technically override every suggestion. The suggestions you see become the decisions you make.

Skill atrophy: Write with AI, your writing skills degrade. Code with AI, your coding skills atrophy. This creates a dependency loop — the worse you get, the more you need the tool.

Preference shaping: AI systems trained on your preferences also form them. Your "own" tastes become partially authored by the system that serves them.

Incremental surrender: This is the most insidious. No single interaction strips agency. Each delegation is rational and beneficial on its own. The erosion is in the aggregate.

Two Autonomies

Independence vs. Self-Governance

Autonomy-as-Independence

Engineering self-sufficiency. The ability to act without constant human direction. Current AI has this to some degree.

  • Multi-step planning
  • Tool use and execution
  • Dynamic replanning on failure
  • State continuity across sessions

Autonomy-as-Moral-Self-Governance

Kantian self-legislation. First-person awareness. The capacity to act for reasons rather than causal chains. Current AI lacks this entirely.

  • Moral understanding
  • First-person awareness
  • Reason-responsive behavior
  • Genuine intentionality

When people worry about AI "going rogue," they conflate the two. AI can act independently without being morally self-governing. It manipulates syntax without grasping semantics — agency without intelligence.

08
8 / 9

This distinction from the philosophy of agentic AI is critical. There are two kinds of autonomy, and people constantly confuse them.

Autonomy-as-independence is engineering — can the system act without me telling it every step? Yes, current AI has this. It plans, executes, replans, and remembers.

Autonomy-as-moral-self-governance is Kantian — can the system understand right and wrong, act for reasons rather than causal chains, take first-person responsibility? No. Current AI lacks this entirely.

Some philosophers argue AI is better understood as "agency without intelligence" — the capacity to act without the capacity to understand. High agency does not imply high comprehension.

When people worry about AI "going rogue," they're worried about moral self-governance. But what we actually have is independence — which is powerful but fundamentally different.

The Takeaway

Delegate Execution

Not Intention

Human agency and AI agency are complementary. The risk is not that AI seizes agency — it's that humans surrender it through convenience.

"You could have infinite time by borrowing from the machines — but only if you're the one deciding what to do with it."

Close on the core message. Delegate execution, not intention.

Human agency — problem, taste, judgment — and AI agency — action, planning, execution — are complementary forces. They amplify each other when the boundary is clear. They erode each other when it blurs.

The risk is not that AI seizes agency. It's that humans surrender it through a thousand rational micro-decisions. Each one individually sensible. The aggregate is a shift in who controls outcomes.

End on Gary's quote about infinite time. It's beautiful and it's true. But it comes with a condition: you have to be the one choosing.
