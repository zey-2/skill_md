# Ride the Models

## The Only Durable Career Strategy in the AI Era

*The models keep moving. The people who stay ahead are the ones moving with them.*

---

## Introduction: We Are Still Figuring This Out

People are still figuring out what they can do with AI. Not the researchers building the models, but the actual users: the people who sit down each morning and try to make something real.

That matters because AI capability is not discovered all at once. It is uncovered task by task. A model may look ordinary in a demo, then suddenly become valuable when a teacher uses it to design a lesson, a lawyer uses it to pressure-test an argument, or an engineer uses it to debug a stubborn system. Every model release reveals capabilities that few people predicted. Every practitioner discovers use cases that the builders may never have imagined. The frontier of what AI can do is not only in a lab. It is wherever a person applies a model to their specific work for the first time.

Dan Shipper, co-founder and CEO of Every, put it directly: "I think the edge of AI is wherever AI meets a real human doing something. The people in San Francisco are making it, but they don't actually know a lot about how to use it."

This article is about the strategy that emerges from that idea. Shipper calls it "ride the models." In his view, it is the only durable career strategy available as AI capabilities accelerate.

## The Problem: Yesterday's Competence Gets Cheaper Every Month

Here is the structural problem: AI models ingest existing data and deploy it at near-zero marginal cost. They make yesterday's human competence cheap.

A skill that took years to develop, such as writing marketing copy, building a basic web app, summarizing legal documents, or creating a data visualization, can now be approximated by a model in seconds. This is not a judgment about whether the model's output is as good as an expert's. It is a statement about economics. When something becomes abundant, its price falls.

That creates a fork in the road. One path is to use AI to do the same old work faster: more copy, more code, more summaries, more charts. That can help for a while, but it keeps you competing on a bar the models are already learning to clear. The more interesting path is to use AI to discover work that was previously too slow, too expensive, or too complex to attempt.

The people who built careers on those skills face a choice:

1. **Compete on the old bar** and watch their leverage erode as models catch up.
2. **Find a new bar** that the models cannot yet reach.

Option two is not a one-time fix. It is a permanent stance. As soon as you find a new bar, the models will start closing in on it too. The gap between what models can do and what skilled humans can do with those models is a moving target, and the only way to stay ahead is to keep moving.

## The Strategy: Ride the Models

Shipper's prescription is deceptively simple:

> "The only thing you need to do is ride the models. Use them for whatever it is that you do. When new models come out, you're trying them and figuring out, 'Okay, how can I use their new powers?' instead of ignoring it because it makes you afraid."

"Ride the models" is not a specific workflow or tool recommendation. It is a stance:

* **When a new model arrives, turn the rock over again.** Test whether it can now do what it could not do last time.
* **Apply it to whatever you care about**, including your job, side projects, and personal tasks.
* **Stay curious and playful rather than afraid.** FOMO-driven adoption is brittle. Genuine enjoyment of the discovery process is what sustains engagement.
* **Find your "moment of joy"**: the first time AI does something surprising and useful for you personally. That is what keeps you riding.

## Practical Tactics

How do you actually ride the models in practice? Start with a few simple habits that make experimentation repeatable rather than random.

### 1. Play with Every New Release

When a new model drops, do not read the changelog first. Use it. Take tasks that previously failed or produced mediocre results and try them again. The gap between "could not do it" and "can do it well enough" can close surprisingly fast.

### 2. Use Both Major Platforms

Different models have different strengths. OpenAI and Anthropic models are not interchangeable. Do not lock into one. Understanding what each platform does well is part of the rider's toolkit.

### 3. Apply It to Your Actual Work

The rider's advantage comes from applying models to specific, real situations rather than toy projects. Try new workflows in Claude Code, Codex, or other tools. See how the work surface changes. Find what clicks.

### 4. Share Discoveries

Collective exploration accelerates individual learning. When you find something that works, share it. When others share, learn from them. The community of riders moves faster than any individual.

### 5. Re-Test Your Benchmarks

Shipper's senior engineer benchmark is one example. Whatever your domain, create a way to measure whether a new model has crossed a threshold. When it does, raise the bar.

## The Senior Engineer Benchmark: A Concrete Example

Shipper illustrates this with a specific example. He built a test application called Proof through vibe coding, then asked models to rewrite it from first principles. The benchmark tested whether the models could reconstruct the application cleanly, reason through its design, and produce working software at the level expected from an experienced engineer. The scoring was stark: models scored 30 out of 100, while human senior engineers scored in the 80s and 90s.

Then GPT-5.5 arrived and scored 62, a 30-point jump in a single model generation. That jump matters because it shows how quickly a task that looked safely outside model range can become partially automated once the next model crosses a capability threshold.

But the deeper insight is not the score itself. It is this: **it would be easy to change the benchmark to zero out the current model once it saturates the current framing.** Humans can always reframe the problem at a higher level. Benchmark saturation does not equal capability parity; it means the human has found a new bar.

This is the core of riding the models. You do not compete with the model on its current capabilities. You use the model to reach somewhere it has not been yet.

## Why This Works: The Structural Gap

The reason riding the models works is not luck. It is structural:

1. **Models always trail the people pushing them into novel territory.** A model is trained on past data. It cannot know about the way you used it yesterday, at least not until that usage becomes data for the next training run.
2. **By then, riders have already moved further ahead.** The people who constantly experiment are always at the edge of what the model can do, and that edge is always shifting.
3. **The gap closes only when novel usage becomes training data.** But novel usage is, by definition, always being generated by the people at the frontier.

This creates a durable advantage. Not because the models are weak, but because the people who ride them are always creating new competence faster than the models can absorb it.

## Who Gains the Edge

The frontier is no longer defined mainly by geography or proximity to the companies building the models. It is defined by usage density: how often people test new capabilities against real problems, how quickly they notice what has changed, and how willing they are to rebuild their workflows around those changes.

That is why the advantage can appear in unexpected places. A product manager who uses a new model every week to prototype customer research may move faster than a team sitting closer to the model labs but waiting for official playbooks. A teacher who keeps testing lesson design, feedback, and assessment workflows may discover practical capabilities before an education software vendor packages them. An engineer who repeatedly applies models to live debugging, documentation, and system design builds a private map of what the tools can and cannot yet do.

The same logic applies to organizations. Companies that give employees safe access to current models, room to experiment, and channels to share discoveries will compound learning across the workforce. Companies that restrict experimentation too heavily may protect against some risk, but they also slow the formation of judgment. They end up with policies before intuition, and process before practice.

Accessibility makes this shift more important. Advanced models are not reserved only for the largest institutions. They are available to individuals and small teams quickly enough that advantage can come from disciplined use rather than institutional scale.

So the real question is not whether you are in San Francisco, Brooklyn, Singapore, or anywhere else. The question is whether you are close to the work, close to the models, and using both often enough for new possibilities to reveal themselves.

## What Companies Get Wrong

Many organizations handicap their employees by restricting access to the latest models. Approval workflows. Mandated tools. Waiting for "enterprise-ready" versions that often lag behind consumer releases.

Shipper's advice is blunt: "Maybe you have to do it on your own time."

The competitive advantage of riding the models compounds over time. Waiting for organizational permission puts you behind. The people who experiment on their own time, through nights, weekends, and personal projects, build intuition that translates directly to professional advantage.

This is not a recommendation to violate company policy. It is an observation: organizations that enable their employees to ride the models will accelerate adoption; those that restrict them will slow it. Individuals who ride the models will outperform those who wait.

## Model Selection: Reach for the Right Tool

Felix Rieseberg, the engineer behind Claude Cowork, provides a practical heuristic for choosing between model tiers:

**Reach for Opus when you do not yet know what you are asking for. Sonnet is sufficient for well-scoped problems.**

The distinction is not about technical complexity but about problem decomposition. A lawyer's, accountant's, or creative professional's value lies partly in reinterpreting what the client actually needs; Opus excels at that reframing work. For concrete tasks, such as reading a floor plan, adding units, or making a new one, Sonnet 4.6 is "very very good" and rarely bumps into its ceiling.

For most people, Rieseberg suggests not thinking about model selection too deeply because Sonnet handles the majority of daily tasks well. The model choice becomes relevant when the problem itself is ambiguous and the value is in figuring out what question to ask.

This itself is a riding-the-models insight: the rider knows not just what the models can do, but when to reach for which one.

## The Future Horizon

Shipper has a useful model for thinking about the future:

> "Our intuitions about the future are like the intuitions people had in the Middle Ages about what happened at the end of the horizon. Are there dragons? Does it drop off into nothingness? What happens is you get there and you're like, there are some really cool things, there's some not cool things, and it's just another horizon."

Until you reach that place where you are starting to see it, it is important not to let your mind get away from you by telling stories that sound real in the moment but later become much more complex. "Everything has changed and nothing has," Shipper says. "Once you get there, you start seeing that, oh yeah, this is a real thing."

Riding the models is how you get there: not by predicting the future, but by being among the first to see it when it arrives.

## Conclusion: Start Riding

The people who thrive in the AI era will not be the ones who predicted which model would win. They will be the ones who used every model as it arrived, found what it could do, applied it to their work, and moved on to the next frontier before everyone else caught up.

The models will keep getting better. That is certain. The question is whether you are on top of them when they do.

Ride the models. Turn over the rocks. Stay playful. The edge is wherever you are, right now, doing something real with AI for the first time.
