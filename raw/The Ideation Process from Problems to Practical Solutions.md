Ideation is the process of generating, developing, and refining ideas to solve a problem or create something new. It is often associated with brainstorming, but effective ideation involves much more than producing a long list of suggestions. It combines creativity, research, evaluation, experimentation, and learning.

A well-structured ideation process helps individuals and teams avoid rushing into the first solution that comes to mind. Instead, it encourages them to understand the problem, explore different possibilities, and test the most promising ideas before investing significant time or resources.

The process can be summarised as:

**Understand → Explore → Generate → Combine → Evaluate → Prototype → Learn**

## 1. Define the Challenge

The ideation process begins with a clearly defined challenge.

A vague problem statement such as:

> We need a better application.

provides little direction. A more useful statement would be:

> How might we help busy parents organise their children’s school activities with less manual tracking?

A clear challenge identifies the target user, the problem they face, and the desired outcome. It should also consider practical constraints such as cost, time, technology, and available resources.

Before generating ideas, it is useful to ask:

- Who experiences the problem?
    
- What are they trying to achieve?
    
- What difficulties do they currently face?
    
- What constraints must be considered?
    
- What would a successful outcome look like?
    

This stage is important because a poorly defined problem can lead to an impressive solution that addresses the wrong need.

## 2. Gather Insights

Once the challenge is defined, focus on understanding the people, environment, and processes connected to the problem.

Insights may be gathered through:

- User interviews
    
- Observation
    
- Surveys
    
- Feedback and complaint records
    
- Competitor analysis
    
- Workflow reviews
    
- Usage data
    

The goal is not merely to collect information but to uncover patterns, frustrations, behaviours, and unmet needs.

For example, parents may already use calendars, messaging applications, and reminder tools. However, school information may arrive through several channels, making it difficult to track deadlines and required actions.

This observation could lead to the following insight:

> Parents do not necessarily need another calendar. They need a reliable way to convert scattered school messages into clear actions and reminders.

Strong insights provide a foundation for more relevant ideas.

## 3. Reframe the Problem

A problem can often be viewed from several perspectives. Reframing helps uncover opportunities that may not be visible in the original problem statement.

One common method is to create **“How might we”** questions.

For example:

- How might we reduce the effort required to capture school events?
    
- How might we automatically identify dates and actions in school messages?
    
- How might we make important deadlines more difficult to miss?
    
- How might we improve coordination between family members?
    

Each question directs attention towards a slightly different part of the problem.

Reframing is valuable because the way a problem is described influences the types of solutions that are generated. A narrow question may produce only minor improvements, while a broader or differently framed question may reveal new possibilities.

## 4. Generate a Wide Range of Ideas

The idea-generation stage is the divergent part of the process. Its purpose is to explore many possible solutions before deciding which ones are practical.

During this stage, quantity is initially more important than quality. Judging ideas too early may discourage creativity and cause potentially useful concepts to be discarded before they are properly developed.

Several methods can support idea generation:

### Brainwriting

Participants write down their ideas individually before sharing them with others. This reduces the influence of dominant voices and gives quieter participants more opportunity to contribute.

### Mind Mapping

A central problem is placed in the middle of a page, and related ideas, features, causes, and solutions are connected around it. This helps reveal relationships between concepts.

### SCAMPER

SCAMPER encourages people to examine an existing product, service, or process by asking whether they can:

- Substitute something
    
- Combine elements
    
- Adapt an idea
    
- Modify or enlarge it
    
- Put it to another use
    
- Eliminate unnecessary parts
    
- Reverse or rearrange the process
    

### Crazy 8s

Participants sketch eight ideas within a short period. The time constraint encourages rapid thinking and reduces over-analysis.

### Worst Possible Idea

Participants intentionally generate impractical or undesirable solutions. They then examine why those ideas are poor and whether reversing or modifying them can produce something valuable.

### Analogy Thinking

A similar problem is examined in another industry or context. For example, a family reminder system might borrow ideas from airline checklists, project-management software, or hospital alert systems.

At this stage, unusual ideas should be welcomed. Even an unrealistic suggestion may contain one useful feature that can be combined with another concept.

## 5. Group and Combine Ideas

Once ideas have been generated, organise them into themes.

For a school-activity management solution, ideas might be grouped into categories such as:

|Theme|Example ideas|
|---|---|
|Information capture|Screenshot extraction, email parsing, voice input|
|Reminders|Calendar integration, mobile notifications, Telegram alerts|
|Family coordination|Shared tasks, confirmations, role assignment|
|Artificial intelligence|Message summarisation, deadline detection, priority scoring|

Grouping ideas helps reveal duplication, patterns, and complementary features.

The strongest concept is often not a single original idea. It may be a combination of several smaller ideas. For example, message extraction, family task assignment, and calendar reminders could be merged into one integrated solution.

Combining ideas allows creativity to move from isolated suggestions towards a more complete concept.

## 6. Evaluate and Prioritise

After exploring a broad range of possibilities, the process moves into the convergent stage. The aim is to identify which ideas deserve further development.

Ideas can be evaluated using criteria such as:

- User value
    
- Technical feasibility
    
- Cost
    
- Implementation time
    
- Strategic relevance
    
- Risk
    
- Differentiation
    
- Potential impact
    
- Ease of testing
    

A simple scoring model may be used:

**Idea score = User value + Feasibility + Strategic fit − Risk**

Another useful tool is the impact-effort matrix.

||Low effort|High effort|
|---|---|---|
|High impact|Prioritise|Investigate carefully|
|Low impact|Optional quick wins|Usually avoid|

Evaluation should be based on evidence rather than personal preference. The most exciting idea is not always the most useful one. A simpler solution may provide greater value if it solves the user’s main problem quickly and reliably.

## 7. Develop the Selected Idea into a Concept

A selected idea should then be described clearly enough for others to understand and assess.

A concept description should explain:

- Who the target user is
    
- What problem is being addressed
    
- How the solution works
    
- What benefit it provides
    
- What assumptions it depends on
    
- What risks or limitations exist
    
- How it differs from current alternatives
    

For example:

> The proposed solution is a tool that reads school announcements, identifies important dates and required actions, asks parents to confirm the extracted information, and adds approved items to a shared family calendar.

This description turns an abstract idea into a concept that can be discussed, visualised, and tested.

## 8. Build a Lightweight Prototype

A prototype is a simplified representation of the proposed solution. Its purpose is not to create a finished product but to test whether the idea is understandable, useful, and workable.

Depending on the concept, a prototype might be:

- A paper sketch
    
- A clickable interface
    
- A spreadsheet
    
- A simple Python script
    
- A no-code application
    
- A role-play demonstration
    
- A manually operated service
    
- A landing page
    

The prototype should focus on the most important assumptions.

For example, before building a complete artificial-intelligence-based family organiser, a team could test whether parents find automatically extracted school deadlines useful. A simple prototype might allow users to upload a message and review the dates identified by the system.

This approach reduces cost and allows weaknesses to be discovered early.

## 9. Test with Real Users

Prototypes should be tested with people who genuinely experience the problem.

During testing, users can be asked:

- What do you think this solution does?
    
- Which part is most useful?
    
- What is confusing?
    
- What would prevent you from using it?
    
- How do you currently solve this problem?
    
- What would make this solution more valuable?
    

Observation is often more informative than direct feedback. A user may say that a feature is useful but struggle to understand or operate it. Behaviour can reveal problems that users may not clearly express.

Feedback should be used to test assumptions, not merely to collect approval. Statements such as “It looks good” provide less evidence than seeing a user complete a task successfully.

## 10. Refine, Pivot, or Stop

Following testing, the results determine the most appropriate course of action.

The team may choose to:

- **Refine** the idea if the basic concept works but needs improvement.
    
- **Pivot** if the problem is valid but the proposed solution is unsuitable.
    
- **Expand** the concept if testing reveals a larger opportunity.
    
- **Stop** if the idea does not provide enough value or is not feasible.
    

Stopping an idea is not necessarily a failure. Identifying a weak concept early prevents greater losses later.

Ideation is therefore an iterative process rather than a straight line. Testing often reveals new information about the original problem, leading to better questions and improved ideas.

The cycle may continue as follows:

**Problem → Ideas → Prototype → Evidence → Improved understanding → Better ideas**

## Conclusion

Effective ideation combines imaginative thinking with structured decision-making. Creativity is needed to explore possibilities, while research and testing are needed to determine which possibilities are worth pursuing.

The process begins with understanding the problem and the people affected by it. It then expands through research, reframing, and idea generation before narrowing through evaluation and prioritisation. Promising concepts are converted into prototypes, tested with real users, and improved using evidence.

The key principle is to **diverge widely before converging carefully**. Generating many ideas increases the range of possible solutions, while systematic evaluation ensures that the selected idea is practical, relevant, and valuable.

Ideation is not about finding a perfect idea immediately. It is about creating a disciplined path from uncertainty to a solution that can be tested and improved.
